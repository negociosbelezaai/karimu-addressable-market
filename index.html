Purpose of this report

This study sizes Karimu's Total Addressable Market (TAM): the global universe of rural communities living in extreme poverty whose profile matches the operating model Karimu has already proven in Babati, Tanzania. It exists to answer two questions:

  • Where could Karimu expand? A ranked, filtered list of countries — and, one level down, districts — where the same intervention model is most likely to apply, so that geographic expansion is driven by data rather than opportunity alone.
  • How large is the problem Karimu addresses? A defensible number to communicate to boards, donors and investors about the scale of rural extreme poverty that Karimu's model can, in principle, reach.
What this report is — and is not. It is a market-sizing ruler: it measures how big the opportunity is and how it is distributed across countries and districts. It is not an operational site-selection map: it does not claim that every estimated community exists with a specific name and boundary, nor that Karimu should enter any particular country. Site selection is a later phase that requires exhaustive, on-the-ground enumeration — already piloted in Babati (Tanzania) and Baguia (Timor-Leste).

01

Where we come from: 3 communities, ~47k people

Karimu has worked since 2008 in three wards (the smallest unit of local government in Tanzania) of Babati District, Manyara Region: Arri, Dabil and Ayalagaya. Together they hold roughly 47,360 inhabitants (2022 census) — this is the size of community where Karimu's model already works in practice: small enough for direct intervention (school, health post, savings group), large enough to have public data and its own local leadership.

02

Conditions required for this methodology to hold

The estimate is valid only where the following assumptions are true. Each is a deliberate scope decision, not an oversight — they define the boundary of what the number means.

  • The target-unit size is transferable. The ~15,000-inhabitant community size was calibrated on real data from a single country (Tanzania, 4,102 enumerated wards). The community count assumes communities in the other 42 countries are of comparable scale. If they are systematically larger or smaller, the community count scales inversely — the people count does not change.
  • Karimu's model targets rural poverty. The intervention (school, health post, savings group) is assumed to apply to rural communities below the poverty line. Urban poverty is excluded by design, so the addressable market is the rural share of the poor, not the total poor.
  • The country must allow an NGO to operate. Approximated by two gates: (a) no active armed conflict, and (b) a corruption-perception level not drastically worse than Tanzania, the proven operating baseline. Both are risk proxies, not guarantees of operability.
  • Usable public data must exist. At country level (poverty rate + rural share) to size the market, and at district level (administrative boundaries + population) to later locate it. Where district population was missing, it was estimated from national averages (flagged in the source data).
  • The poverty line is US$3.00/day (2021 PPP). The World Bank's line for lower-middle-income countries (June 2025 revision). A different line would move every figure.
  • Rural/urban disparity is measured where possible, modeled otherwise. For 12 African countries the rural share of poverty was verified against official household surveys; for the rest it is a medium-confidence estimate that assumes the rural/urban gap is stable.

Step 1 · Rural-poverty screen → 53 countries

Material rural population living below US$3.00/day (2021 PPP)

Metric: estimated number of rural people below the US$3.00/day (2021 PPP) poverty line = national population × national poverty rate × rural share of the poor.

Threshold: a material rural-poor population at the scale of Karimu's model. Micro-populations (e.g. Djibouti, Laos, Guinea, The Gambia) are dropped because their total rural-poor count is immaterial to market scale, even though they remain poor countries.

Source: World Bank poverty data (June 2025 revision), using the US$3.00/day (2021 PPP) line for lower-middle-income countries.

Why screen on poverty, not country income: the screen targets rural poverty directly, wherever it occurs — not whether a country is classified as "poor" overall. Country-income labels would discard nations that are not themselves low-income yet hold enormous rural poverty, such as India (~112M rural poor), Kenya (~14M), Bangladesh (~9.5M) and Brazil (~4M). Those populations are squarely within Karimu's model and are captured here.

Step 3 · Governance screen → 43 countries

Corruption perception not drastically worse than Tanzania

Metric: Corruption Perceptions Index (CPI) 2025 score, measured as the gap versus Tanzania (CPI = 40, Karimu's proven operating baseline).

Threshold: exclude countries whose CPI is ≥20 points below Tanzania (i.e. CPI ≤ 20). The cut is deliberately conservative — it removes only the two most severe cases, while countries with a smaller governance gap (e.g. Uganda, Bangladesh, Honduras, Eswatini) remain in the market, since a CPI moderately below Tanzania's still describes an environment where an NGO can operate.

Source: Transparency International CPI 2025 (via Wikipedia, captured 2026-10-13).

04

Market size

Across the 43 countries, 419.5 million people live below the US$3.00/day international poverty line (2021 PPP, World Bank). Of that total, using the measured rural-vs-urban poverty disparity from national household surveys, 313.7 million (74.8%) live in rural areas — the core of Karimu's addressable market.

Total poor (rural + urban, 43 countries)419.5M
...of which, rural313.7M · 74.8%
India alone concentrates 35.7% of the global market: 112 million rural poor in a single country. This does not mean India is necessarily Karimu's next stop, but it sizes the real scale of the global problem the organization addresses. Read without India: the other 42 countries hold 201.7M rural poor across 15,593 districts.

How to read these numbers

Explicit about what is primary data and what is estimate.

  • Inclusion criterion: rural poverty below US$3.00/day (2021 PPP) — the World Bank line for lower-middle-income countries, June 2025 revision, applied to all countries. Armed conflict verified against World Bank FCS FY2025 and the UCDP/PRIO Armed Conflict Dataset v26.1 (Uppsala University), threshold ≥25 battle deaths/year. Governance measured by Transparency International CPI 2025.
  • Rural-poverty estimates derive from World Bank poverty data (share below US$3/day per country). For 12 African countries (Tanzania, Malawi, Zambia, Angola, Rwanda, Benin, Senegal, Sierra Leone, Lesotho, Liberia, Madagascar, and Nepal in Asia) the rural/urban disparity was verified against official household surveys (HBS, IOF, EHCVM, IHS, LCMS, NLSS); for the rest it is a medium-confidence World Bank estimate.
  • Data provenance caveat: the country-level figures are a compilation of World Bank poverty data rather than a direct per-country World Bank API pull. A manual consistency check confirmed India, Kenya and Bangladesh; a full API re-verification of the largest countries is the recommended next step before any investment decision.
  • Community size (15k inhabitants) was calibrated on real data from one country only (Tanzania, 4,102 enumerated wards). Not re-validated for the other 42 — communities elsewhere may be larger or smaller.
  • District identifiers: 41/43 countries use official UN/OCHA P-codes. India: constructed IDs (IND-{ST}-{SEQ}), no bulk LGD codes available. Peru: INEI ubigeo, official. Bhutan: ISO 3166-2:BT, official.
  • India distorts the total: 112M (35.7% of TAM) in a single country, and the community count follows the same proportion (82,392 of 223,467). Alternative read without India: 201.7M rural poor · 141,075 communities · 42 countries.
